# Incident communications template

This template is used when a fault is customer-visible. It is deliberately short: a communications note that
takes longer to write than the incident lasts is worse than no note at all.

## Severity classes

| Class | Definition | First note | Update cadence |
|---|---|---|---|
| S1 | Customer-visible loss of a primary service | within fifteen minutes | every thirty minutes |
| S2 | Degraded service with a workaround | within one hour | every two hours |
| S3 | Internal impact only | same business day | at resolution |
| S4 | Cosmetic or documentation fault | next business day | none |

The class is set by the incident commander, not by the person who noticed the fault, and it is re-set when
the impact changes. A downgrade is announced as loudly as an upgrade; silently reclassifying an incident is
the single most common complaint raised in the postmortem review.

## Structure of a note

The first paragraph says what customers can observe, in their terms. The second says what is being done and
by whom. The third, when known, says when the next update will follow. Anything else — hypotheses, internal
tooling names, the names of engineers — is left out of the customer-facing note and recorded in the internal
timeline instead.

Internal notes carry more detail. They name the working theory, the person testing it, and the two or three
diagnostics already excluded. An internal note that lists every alarm without saying which ones were ruled
out costs the next responder the same investigation twice.

## Postmortem

A postmortem is written for every S1 and for any S2 that repeated within the quarter. It is blameless in the
narrow sense that it names systems and decisions rather than individuals, and it ends with no more than
three actions, each with an owner and a date. Postmortems with ten actions are treated as having none: the
review samples whether the actions were completed, and a list nobody finishes scores worse than a short one
that closes.

## Escalation touchpoints

The communications owner and the incident commander are different people for S1 incidents, so that one can
concentrate on the fault while the other handles the outside world. Handover between them is verbal and
recorded in the timeline, never implied. If the incident outlives two shift changes, the communications owner
writes a shift summary that a newcomer can read in under a minute without asking a question.
